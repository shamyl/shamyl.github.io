Pakistan's first nationally coordinated artificial intelligence research hub focused on healthcare is quietly taking shape at the Lahore University of Management Sciences. The National AI Hub for Maternal, Newborn and Child Health, established at LUMS with funding from the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, has spent 2026 building partnerships with the federal Ministry of Health, the governments of Sindh, Punjab, and Balochistan, and Aga Khan University. The Hub's director, Dr. Maryam Mustafa, is also the creator of Awaaz-e-Sehat, a voice-based AI platform that lets pregnant women in Pakistan build and carry their antenatal records over WhatsApp in Urdu. The initiative represents something Pakistan has rarely attempted: a university-led AI research centre that is simultaneously building deployed tools, advising government policy, and coordinating with provincial health departments across the country.

## In Brief

- LUMS established Pakistan's first National AI Hub for Maternal, Newborn and Child Health (MNCH) with Gates Foundation funding, announced in March 2026
- Dr. Maryam Mustafa, Associate Professor of Computer Science at LUMS, directs the Hub and founded Awaaz-e-Sehat, a voice-based AI maternal health platform
- The Hub has signed MoUs with the federal Ministry of National Health Services, Government of Sindh, Government of Balochistan, and Office of AI Punjab
- In May 2026, the Hub hosted AI x Health 2026, Pakistan's first national convening on AI and health, bringing together over 150 clinicians, researchers, and government officials
- Gates Foundation leaders Dr. Anita Zaidi and Dr. Kalpana Kochhar visited LUMS in October 2026 to review the Hub's progress
- Dr. Mustafa and Dr. Farah Nadeem won a Google Society-Centered AI Award for work on community-governed AI alignment in low-resource contexts
- The Hub is part of Pakistan's broader $1 billion AI investment push announced by Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif in February 2026

## What the National AI Hub Actually Does

The Hub's work sits at the intersection of applied AI research and public health implementation. According to LUMS, the Hub is focused on building and scaling AI tools specifically for maternal, newborn, and child health — not AI for healthcare broadly. This narrowing matters. Pakistan's maternal mortality ratio remains among the highest in South Asia, and the country's health system faces well-documented constraints in rural and low-income urban areas: shortages of trained female health workers, fragmented data systems, and limited diagnostic infrastructure.

The Hub's flagship tool, Awaaz-e-Sehat, illustrates the approach. Rather than building a general-purpose medical chatbot, the platform is designed for a specific user: a pregnant woman in Pakistan who may have limited literacy, no reliable internet connection, and who communicates primarily in Urdu or a regional language. The platform uses speech-based interaction, works through WhatsApp, and allows women to build and carry their own antenatal records. This is AI designed around the constraints of the environment it operates in, not adapted from a Western model after the fact.

According to LUMS, the Hub is also developing AI-enabled risk-stratification models, clinical decision-support systems, and voice-assisted tools for frontline health providers. The partnership with Sindh government and Aga Khan University, signed in October 2026, specifically mentions rolling out these tools to support frontline providers delivering maternal and child health services in the province.

## The Partnership Architecture

What makes the Hub structurally significant is its network of government partnerships. Between July and November 2026, the Hub signed MoUs with four distinct government entities:

**Federal Ministry of National Health Services, Regulations and Coordination (MoNHSRC).** Signed on July 20, 2026, this MoU provides a framework for collaboration in research, innovation, evidence generation, policy engagement, and capacity building. The federal partnership gives the Hub a national mandate and a pathway to influence health policy at the central level.

**Government of Balochistan.** Signed on August 19, 2026, this partnership focuses on identifying opportunities where AI-enabled solutions can strengthen health systems across Balochistan — Pakistan's largest and least resourced province by most health indicators. The Balochistan delegation was led by Mr. Mujeeb Ur Rehman, Secretary, Health Department.

**Government of Sindh and Aga Khan University.** Signed on October 15, 2026, this three-institution partnership will roll out AI-enabled risk-stratification models, clinical decision-support systems, and voice-assisted tools in Sindh. The signing took place at the office of Dr. Azra Fazal Pechuho, Sindh Minister for Health and Population Welfare.

**Office of AI Punjab.** Also signed on October 15, 2026, this MoU connects the Hub with Punjab's emerging provincial AI agenda. The signing brought together Khawaja Imran Nazir, Punjab Minister for Health and Population, and Ali Dar, Advisor to the Chief Minister on AI and Special Initiatives. This partnership is particularly notable because it links a university research centre with a provincial government's dedicated AI office — a governance structure that did not exist in Pakistan until recently.

The Gates Foundation's involvement provides both funding and international credibility. Dr. Anita Zaidi, President of the Gender Equality Division, and Dr. Kalpana Kochhar, Interim President of the Global Policy & Advocacy Division, visited LUMS on October 15, 2026, to review the Hub's work first-hand. According to LUMS, conversations centred on "turning AI research into practical solutions that work within Pakistan's health system."

## AI x Health 2026: The First National Convening

On May 16, 2026, the Hub hosted AI x Health 2026 at LUMS — described as Pakistan's first national convening on AI and health. The event brought together over 150 clinicians, AI researchers, startup founders, frontline implementers, and government health officials from all four provinces and the Federal Ministry.

Fifteen organisations presented active tools and pilots, including Awaaz-e-Sehat, Childlife Foundation, Vital Pakistan Trust, Marham, Sehat Kahani, Oladoc, CERP, SHINE Humanity Pakistan, Viamo, NUST, and Aibers Health. The convening was organised around a practical question: what will it actually take for AI to improve health outcomes for women and children in Pakistan?

A moderated panel with practitioners from PHC Global, Vital Pakistan Trust, Marham, and SPHERE Pakistan identified structural barriers that are well-known but rarely discussed in AI policy circles: fragmented health data systems, the absence of a regulatory framework for digital health, and the persistent tendency to build solutions in isolation. Vice Chancellor Dr. Ali Cheema's closing remarks underscored that AI "is only as effective as the governance and infrastructure around it."

## Why This Matters for Pakistan's AI Strategy

The National AI Hub exists within a broader national AI push. In February 2026, Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif pledged $1 billion in AI investment by 2030 during the inauguration of AI Indus Week in Islamabad. Pakistan's current AI computing infrastructure, as noted by Tech Policy Press, centres on a single AI-optimised data centre in Karachi with roughly 3,000 GPUs — modest by global standards but a starting point. The government has also established a National AI Taskforce and the National Center of Artificial Intelligence to coordinate research and deployment.

The LUMS Hub matters within this strategy for two reasons.

First, it demonstrates a model that Pakistan has struggled to implement at scale: university research that connects directly to government service delivery. Most Pakistani AI initiatives have been either academic exercises with limited real-world deployment or commercial products with limited research rigor. The Hub's combination of Gates Foundation funding, LUMS research capacity, and provincial government partnerships creates a pipeline from research to implementation that is rare in the Pakistani context.

Second, the Hub's focus on voice-based, low-connectivity, local-language AI tools addresses a genuine gap. Most AI healthcare tools are designed for high-resource settings with reliable internet, English-language interfaces, and formal electronic health records. Pakistan's health system has none of these at scale. The Hub's approach — building for the constraints of the environment rather than importing solutions designed for different contexts — is the kind of applied AI strategy that could produce tools with genuine public health impact.

The Hub's work at LUMS is happening alongside several parallel developments in Pakistan's technology and education ecosystem. The government's INSPIRE semiconductor program is building a three-cluster university network to train 7,200 semiconductor professionals by 2030, as we examined in our coverage of [Pakistan's semiconductor education initiative](/blog/pakistan-semiconductor-inspire-program-2026/). Google's partnership with the Ministry of IT includes an AI Centre of Excellence in Islamabad and free Gemini access for students, which we analysed in our [Google Pakistan MoU breakdown](/blog/google-pakistan-mou-ai-gemini-students-digital-economy/). And Pakistan's IT exports continue to grow, reaching $1.25 billion in Q1 FY2027, as we tracked in our [IT exports quarterly analysis](/blog/pakistan-it-exports-q1-fy2027-freelance-record-growth/).

What connects these developments is a shift from viewing AI purely as an economic opportunity to treating it as a public infrastructure challenge. The LUMS Hub embodies this shift. It is not building AI products for commercial exit. It is building AI systems that need to work within government health delivery, which means they need to be reliable, locally appropriate, and integrated into systems that are often analog or fragmented.

## Product Builder's Perspective

From a product-building perspective, the Hub's approach offers several lessons that extend beyond healthcare.

**Designing for constraints produces better products.** Awaaz-e-Sehat works through WhatsApp, uses speech rather than text input, operates in Urdu, and functions in low-connectivity environments. These are not limitations — they are design decisions that make the product usable by its intended audience. For STEAM educators and education technology builders, the same principle applies: tools designed for the actual environment of use, not an idealised version of it, are the ones that get adopted. This is something we have consistently found while [building LearnOSTEAM](/projects/learnosteam) — the platform that works in a Pakistani school with intermittent electricity and shared devices is fundamentally different from one designed for a 1:1 device classroom with reliable broadband.

**University-government partnerships need clear roles.** The Hub's MoUs specify what each party brings: LUMS provides AI research and tool development; government provides health system access, data, and policy alignment. This division is simple but important. Many university-government partnerships in Pakistan have failed because roles were ambiguous or because universities attempted to play roles they were not equipped for.

**Funding structure shapes research direction.** Gates Foundation funding comes with specific priorities — maternal, newborn, and child health — and the Hub's research agenda reflects those priorities. This is neither good nor bad, but it is worth noting. The Hub is not a general-purpose AI research centre. Its scope is defined by its funder's mission. For Pakistani technology leaders considering similar initiatives, the question is whether funder-driven research agendas align with national priorities or whether they create gaps in the overall research landscape.

**Community-governed AI alignment is a genuine innovation.** The Google Society-Centered AI Award given to Dr. Mustafa and Dr. Nadeem is for work that lets communities — women health workers, teachers, patients, and adolescent girls — evaluate AI systems against criteria they set themselves. This is not the standard approach to AI safety, which typically involves technical benchmarks or expert review panels. It is an attempt to build accountability mechanisms that are grounded in the communities AI systems serve, which is particularly relevant in Pakistan where Western AI benchmarks routinely fail to capture local linguistic, cultural, and social context.

## What to Watch Next

Several questions will determine whether the National AI Hub becomes a lasting institution or a time-bound project.

**Deployment evidence.** The Hub has signed partnerships and built tools. The next test is whether those tools are actually deployed in government health facilities and whether they demonstrably improve health outcomes. As of November 2026, the Hub has not published clinical evaluation results or deployment scale data. The Sindh and Punjab partnerships, both signed in October 2026, are the most likely candidates for early deployment evidence.

**Sustainability beyond Gates funding.** Gates Foundation grants typically run for defined periods. The Hub will eventually need to demonstrate impact to secure renewed funding or transition to government support. Pakistan's federal and provincial health budgets are constrained, and AI in healthcare is not yet a line item in most health department budgets.

**Regulatory framework for digital health.** The AI x Health 2026 panel identified the absence of a regulatory framework for digital health as a structural barrier. Without one, AI tools deployed in Pakistan's health system operate in a governance vacuum. The Hub's federal MoU with MoNHSRC includes policy engagement, but turning that into actual regulation will require legislative work that extends beyond any single research centre's mandate.

**Provincial implementation variance.** Pakistan's four provinces have vastly different health system capacities. A tool that works in Punjab's relatively better-resourced health infrastructure may not work the same way in Balochistan. The Hub's partnerships with all three provinces that have signed MoUs are a recognition of this challenge, but implementation will reveal whether a single set of AI tools can adapt to genuinely different health system contexts.

## Conclusion

Pakistan's National AI Hub at LUMS is one of the most coherent attempts to build applied AI for public health in a developing country. It combines a clear focus — maternal, newborn, and child health — with a realistic delivery model that pairs university research with government health systems. The partnerships with four government entities, the Gates Foundation's backing, and the practical orientation of tools like Awaaz-e-Sehat suggest an initiative that understands the environment it operates in.

The open questions are about deployment, evaluation, and sustainability. Partnerships and MoUs are the starting line, not the finish. What matters now is whether the Hub's tools reach frontline health workers, whether they improve outcomes for mothers and children, and whether the model can sustain itself beyond the current funding cycle. For Pakistan's broader AI strategy, the Hub is a test case for whether university-led, mission-focused AI research can deliver public impact at scale.
