Punjab's government has decided to introduce artificial intelligence as a formal subject from Class 1 through Class 12, making it the most ambitious AI-in-schools proposal in Pakistan's history. Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz Sharif directed officials to prepare the curriculum and establish supporting laboratories during a meeting on education reforms on September 25, 2026, according to ProPakistani. The plan also includes a 25% reduction in textbook volume, QR codes on textbook pages linking to video lessons, and one day per week dedicated to teacher training.

The announcement is significant. Punjab is Pakistan's most populous province, with over 50,000 public schools and millions of enrolled students. If implemented, this would be among the most comprehensive AI education initiatives in any developing-country school system. But the gap between announcing a curriculum and delivering one — especially one spanning twelve grade levels — is enormous. The details that will determine success or failure are not yet public.

## In Brief

- Punjab Chief Minister Maryam Nawaz Sharif directed officials on September 25, 2026, to prepare an AI curriculum for Class 1 through Class 12
- The plan includes establishing AI laboratories in schools and reducing textbook volume by 25%
- New textbooks will carry QR codes linking to supplementary video lessons
- One day per week will be dedicated to teacher training, with textbook authors involved in training delivery
- The government framed the textbook reduction as a shift from rote memorization toward concept-based learning
- No timeline, budget, curriculum framework, or implementation roadmap has been published yet

## What the Announcement Covers

The directive emerged from a special meeting on education reforms chaired by the Chief Minister. According to ProPakistani's reporting, the meeting produced several connected decisions:

**AI as a subject from Class 1 to Class 12.** This is not an elective or an after-school club. It is a proposal to introduce AI as a formal subject across the entire school career, from early primary through intermediate. The Chief Minister also stressed the need for laboratories alongside the curriculum.

**Textbook reform.** Textbook volume will be reduced by 25%, which officials described as supporting a shift from rote memorization toward concept-based learning. New textbooks will include QR codes that students can scan to access video lessons on relevant topics.

**Teacher training.** One day each week will be dedicated to teacher training. Authors of the new textbooks will personally train teachers in their use — a design choice that connects curriculum development directly to classroom delivery.

These three elements are interconnected. An AI curriculum without trained teachers is a document. Teacher training without reformed textbooks perpetuates the same content. QR-coded supplementary materials without reliable internet access in schools are inaccessible to the students who need them most. Each piece depends on the others.

## What the Announcement Does Not Cover

Several critical questions remain unanswered:

**What "AI education" means at each grade level.** AI as a subject for a Class 1 student and AI as a subject for a Class 12 student are fundamentally different things. For young children, AI education could mean understanding that computers can recognize patterns, that algorithms make decisions based on data, and that technology has limitations. For older students, it could involve programming, data analysis, machine learning concepts, and ethical reasoning about AI systems. Without a grade-by-grade framework, "AI from Class 1 to 12" is a slogan, not a curriculum.

**Laboratory infrastructure.** The Chief Minister called for AI laboratories, but Punjab's public schools vary enormously in infrastructure. Many lack reliable electricity, functional computer labs, or internet connectivity. According to the Pakistan Social and Living Standards Measurement (PSLM) survey, significant gaps in school ICT infrastructure persist across the province. An AI lab requires hardware, software, network access, maintenance, and physical security — each of which has cost implications that have not been discussed.

**Teacher capacity.** Pakistan faces a substantial shortage of qualified computer science teachers. Introducing AI as a subject compounds this shortage. Weekly training is a start, but training existing teachers — many of whom do not have computer science backgrounds — to teach AI concepts is a multi-year effort, not a single-day-per-week intervention.

**Curriculum development process.** Who writes the curriculum? Which institutions are involved? Will it be developed by the Punjab Curriculum and Textbook Board (PCTB), the School Education Department, or an external committee? Will industry or university partners contribute? The quality and currency of AI curricula depend heavily on who writes them and how quickly they can be updated.

**Budget and timeline.** No financial allocation or implementation schedule has been announced. Curriculum development, textbook revision, laboratory construction, teacher training, and ongoing support each require sustained funding over multiple years.

## Why This Matters for Pakistan's Education System

Punjab's school system is large enough that its curriculum decisions affect millions of students directly and influence other provinces through precedent. If Punjab successfully introduces AI education across all grades, it creates a template that Sindh, KPK, and Balochistan can adapt. If the initiative stalls — like many well-intentioned education reforms in Pakistan's history — it reinforces skepticism about ambitious policy announcements.

The timing also matters. Pakistan's technology exports crossed $4.5 billion in FY2026, and the federal government has been actively promoting technology as an economic growth engine, as discussed in our analysis of [Pakistan's IT export growth and the FY2026-27 budget](/blog/pakistan-it-exports-2026-budget-freelance-economy/). A workforce that encounters AI concepts from primary school is better prepared for the technology economy than one that first encounters them at university. The [INSPIRE semiconductor program](/blog/pakistan-semiconductor-inspire-program-2026/) similarly targets early pipeline development — but at the university level. Starting AI education in primary school reaches students far earlier.

## Global Context: How Other Countries Are Approaching AI in Schools

Punjab is not alone in attempting to bring AI into classrooms. Several countries have taken different approaches, and the differences are instructive.

**China** introduced AI-related textbooks in primary and secondary schools starting around 2019, with pilot programs in major cities. The Chinese approach focused on practical coding, robotics, and machine learning concepts, but faced challenges in rural areas where infrastructure and teacher expertise lagged. China's Ministry of Education later issued guidelines requiring AI courses at all levels of compulsory education, but implementation quality has varied significantly between urban and rural schools.

**India's Central Board of Secondary Education (CBSE)** introduced artificial intelligence as a skill subject in Classes 9 and 11 starting in 2019, later extending it to Classes 6 through 8 as a module. CBSE's approach was more measured — starting with older students, piloting in selected schools, and developing teacher training modules through partnerships with companies including Intel and IBM. The phased approach allowed for iterative improvement before broader rollout.

**The United Kingdom** introduced AI concepts within its existing computing curriculum rather than as a standalone subject. The National Centre for Computing Education provides training and resources for teachers, but AI is treated as one component of computational thinking rather than a separate discipline.

**United States:** There is no federal AI curriculum mandate. Individual states and districts have introduced AI guidelines at varying paces. The lack of a national framework has led to uneven adoption, with well-resourced districts moving quickly and others not at all.

The pattern across countries is consistent: AI in schools works best when it starts with older students, pilots before scaling, and is backed by sustained teacher training and industry partnerships. Punjab's plan to introduce AI from Class 1 upward — essentially starting everywhere at once — is more ambitious than most of these examples. Ambition is valuable, but it increases execution risk.

## For STEAM Educators and EdTech Builders

For educators and product teams working in Pakistan's education ecosystem, this announcement creates both opportunities and obligations.

If the curriculum is implemented seriously, schools will need content, tools, and platforms that teach AI concepts in age-appropriate ways. Platforms like [LearnOSTEAM](/projects/learnosteam), which already delivers STEAM education across Pakistani schools, are well-positioned to extend their content to include AI modules. But the content must be designed for the reality of Pakistani classrooms — limited devices, intermittent connectivity, and teachers who are themselves learning alongside their students.

The QR code initiative is particularly interesting. Linking physical textbooks to digital video lessons through QR codes is a low-bandwidth approach to blended learning that suits Pakistan's infrastructure constraints. It does not require every student to have a device — a single teacher's phone or a shared classroom tablet can scan the code and display the video. For EdTech product builders, this creates a clear use case: short, curriculum-aligned video lessons that work on low-end Android devices over 3G or weaker connections.

Teacher training is the hardest problem. Pakistan has hundreds of thousands of school teachers. Training even a fraction of them to teach AI concepts — or to use AI-assisted teaching tools — requires a scale of professional development that the current system struggles to deliver. The plan to dedicate one day per week to training is ambitious, but its effectiveness depends on what the training contains, who delivers it, and whether it is sustained over months rather than weeks.

## What Needs to Happen Next

For this initiative to move from announcement to impact, several things need to happen:

**Publish a curriculum framework.** A grade-by-grade outline of what AI education means at each level — from understanding technology's role in daily life in early primary, to basic programming and data concepts in middle school, to machine learning principles and ethical reasoning in secondary school. This document should be public, consultative, and open to input from educators, industry, and universities.

**Pilot before scaling.** Rather than launching across all 50,000+ schools simultaneously, pilot the curriculum in a representative sample of schools — urban and rural, well-resourced and under-resourced — and iterate based on what works. India's CBSE approach of starting with Classes 9 and 11 before expanding is a proven model.

**Build teacher training infrastructure.** One day per week is a time allocation, not a training program. The government needs to specify what teachers will learn, who will teach them, and how their progress will be assessed. Partnerships with universities and EdTech companies could accelerate this.

**Address infrastructure gaps.** AI laboratories require electricity, internet, devices, and maintenance. For schools that lack these basics, a phased approach that prioritizes digital literacy before AI-specific labs would be more realistic.

**Engage the EdTech ecosystem.** Pakistan has a growing EdTech sector with companies and platforms already working in schools. The government does not need to build everything from scratch. Public-private partnerships for content development, teacher training, and platform delivery could reduce both cost and time to impact.

The lesson from the [Salamanca humanoid robot backlash](/blog/humanoid-robot-classroom-salamanca-what-went-wrong/) applies here: introducing technology into schools without adequate preparation, community engagement, or transparency creates backlash that sets the entire field back. Punjab's AI curriculum needs to be built carefully, piloted honestly, and scaled gradually.

## What to Watch

The next indicators of whether this initiative is serious will be:

- Publication of a curriculum framework with grade-level specifics
- Budget allocation in the provincial education budget
- Selection of pilot schools and a phased implementation timeline
- Partnerships with universities, EdTech companies, or international education organizations
- Teacher training program details beyond the weekly time commitment

Until these appear, the announcement is a direction, not a plan. Directions matter — they signal political will and set expectations. But plans are what deliver results in classrooms.
