On November 25, 2026, Pakistan is expected to send its first astronaut into space. The mission will use China's Shenzhou-24 spacecraft to transport a Pakistani payload specialist to the Tiangong space station for a short-duration stay, making Pakistan one of the first countries to send a citizen to orbit through China's human spaceflight program. The announcement came from Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal on October 19, 2026, during the closing ceremony of World Space Week in Narowal.

## In Brief

- Pakistan's first astronaut will launch aboard China's Shenzhou-24 mission on November 25, 2026, traveling to the Tiangong space station as a payload specialist.
- Two Pakistani candidates — Khurram Daud and Muhammad Zeeshan Ali — have been training in China since April 2026; one will be selected for the flight.
- The astronaut will conduct experiments in material science, biotechnology, and life sciences in microgravity.
- SUPARCO and the China Manned Space Agency signed the cooperation agreement in February 2025.
- Pakistan is also collaborating with China on a lunar rover for the Chang'e-8 mission around 2028 and has set a target of landing on the Moon by 2035.
- A Space Museum will be established at the University of Narowal to promote space science education.

## How the Tiangong Mission Works

China's Tiangong ("Heavenly Palace") space station is a modular outpost in low Earth orbit, operating at approximately 340–450 kilometers above Earth. It consists of three main modules: the Tianhe core module (launched April 2021), and two experiment modules — Wentian and Mengtian (launched in 2022). The station typically hosts a three-person crew in six-month rotations, though the current Shenzhou-23 mission, which launched on May 24, 2026, includes one crew member who will remain aboard for a full year — the longest single-mission duration in China's space program history.

The Shenzhou spacecraft is China's crewed vehicle, launched atop a Long March-2F rocket from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwest China. It carries three people: a commander, a pilot, and a payload specialist. The current Shenzhou-23 crew consists of commander Zhu Yangzhu, pilot Zhang Zhiyuan, and payload specialist Lai Ka-ying — the first astronaut from Hong Kong to fly in space.

According to [SatNews Publishers](https://www.satnews.com/china-launches-shenzhou-23-mission-to-tiangong-slated-for-record-year-long-stay/), the Pakistani astronaut will fly aboard the Shenzhou-24 mission later in 2026 and will use the vacated seat of the year-long Shenzhou-23 crew member for the return flight. This means the Pakistani astronaut's visit will be a short-duration mission — likely lasting days to a couple of weeks — rather than a full crew rotation.

## The Two Pakistani Candidates

Two Pakistani candidates were selected for training in China in April 2026: Khurram Daud and Muhammad Zeeshan Ali. According to [ARY News](https://arynews.tv/pakistans-first-astronaut-to-fly-into-space-on-november-25/), both have been undergoing advanced training with Chinese counterparts, and one will be chosen for the November 25 mission.

The selected astronaut will serve as a payload specialist — the same role held by Lai Ka-ying on Shenzhou-23. Payload specialists are not career astronauts in the traditional sense; they are scientists or technical personnel selected for their expertise in specific research domains. For Pakistan, this means the chosen candidate will focus on conducting scientific experiments rather than piloting or station operations.

Limited public information is available about the candidates' backgrounds. According to reporting by the [CPEC Info Portal](https://cpecinfo.com/pakistan-to-launch-first-astronaut-into-space-from-china-on-nov-25-ahsan-iqbal/) citing Radio Pakistan, the training has been conducted under a bilateral program between SUPARCO (Pakistan's Space and Upper Atmosphere Research Commission) and the China Manned Space Agency.

## What Science Will the Pakistani Astronaut Conduct?

According to ARY News, citing officials, the Pakistani astronaut will conduct multiple scientific experiments in microgravity aboard Tiangong. The planned research covers three key fields:

- **Material science**: Studying how materials behave in microgravity, where convection and sedimentation effects are eliminated. This can reveal fundamental properties of alloys, semiconductors, and crystals that are masked by gravity on Earth.
- **Biotechnology**: Experiments may include protein crystallization, cell culture studies, and microbial behavior — all of which behave differently in microgravity and have applications in pharmaceutical development.
- **Life sciences**: Research on the effects of microgravity on biological organisms, which is essential for understanding human health in space and for developing countermeasures for long-duration spaceflight.

Officials have indicated that the findings could contribute to addressing challenges related to climate change and food security — suggesting that some experiments may focus on agricultural science or Earth observation applications relevant to Pakistan's development priorities.

## The China-Pakistan Space Cooperation Timeline

The November 2026 mission is the result of a bilateral agreement that has been developing for over a year:

- **February 2025**: SUPARCO and the China Manned Space Agency signed an agreement to prepare a Pakistani astronaut for a mission to Tiangong. According to [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/china-set-for-its-first-one-year-human-spaceflight-mission-confirms-pakistani-astronaut-flight/), this was confirmed in February 2026 when China announced that the first international astronaut would visit Tiangong aboard Shenzhou-24.
- **February 2025**: In the same month, CNSA (China National Space Administration) and SUPARCO signed a separate MoU for a Pakistani rover to fly on China's Chang'e-8 lunar south pole mission in 2028, as reported by [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/pakistan-rover-to-fly-on-chinas-change-8-lunar-south-pole-mission/). The 35-kilogram rover will carry science payloads for studying lunar soil composition, radiation levels, and plasma properties.
- **April 2026**: The two Pakistani candidates began training in China.
- **October 19, 2026**: Planning Minister Ahsan Iqbal publicly announced the November 25 launch date during World Space Week celebrations in Narowal.

Pakistan's broader space ambitions, as outlined by Iqbal, include a lunar landing by 2035. China's support is expected to play a significant role, building on the countries' existing collaboration under the International Lunar Research Station (ILRS) framework — a China-led lunar base project that Pakistan has joined alongside Russia, Venezuela, Belarus, Azerbaijan, South Africa, Egypt, and others.

## Why This Mission Matters for Pakistan

Pakistan's space program has historically been modest compared to its neighbor India's ISRO, which has achieved Mars orbit insertion, lunar south pole landing, and multiple satellite constellations. Pakistan's SUPARCO has focused primarily on Earth observation and communication satellites, often launched from Chinese facilities. The PRSC-EO3 imaging satellite, launched in 2025, was an indigenous Earth-observation satellite launched from China.

Sending a person to space is categorically different. It signals a shift from satellite operations to human spaceflight — a domain that only a handful of countries have entered. For Pakistan, the implications are:

**Scientific capability**: Microgravity experiments conducted by a Pakistani scientist in orbit provide firsthand data that cannot be obtained through remote or automated systems. The results could feed into Pakistani university research programs and establish the country's first human-spaceflight-era scientific output.

**Inspiration and education**: The mission has obvious value for STEM engagement. A Pakistani astronaut in orbit is a visible, relatable role model for students — similar to how the UAE's Sultan al-Neyadi and Saudi Arabia's Sultan bin Salman have been used to drive interest in science and technology across the Arab world.

**Strategic positioning**: By participating in China's Tiangong program, Pakistan becomes part of an emerging international space cooperation framework. China has opened Tiangong to foreign astronauts, and Pakistan is among the first to participate. This positions Pakistan within the ILRS lunar program as well, where its rover will fly on [Chang'e-8](/blog/space-tech-2026-08-23/) — the successor to the Chang'e-7 lunar south pole mission that launched in August 2026.

## What This Means for STEAM Educators

For educators in Pakistan and across South Asia, the Tiangong mission offers a concrete, contemporary teaching opportunity. Space science has always been a powerful hook for student engagement, but the abstraction of NASA missions or European Space Agency programs can feel distant from a Pakistani classroom. A Pakistani astronaut aboard a space station — launched by a country that Pakistan shares a border with — makes the concept of space exploration tangible in a way that foreign missions rarely achieve.

At [LearnOBots](https://learnobots.com), satellite technology and space science have consistently been among the most engaging topics for students. The Tiangong mission creates new teaching opportunities: the physics of orbital mechanics, the biology of microgravity, the engineering of spacecraft docking, and the international cooperation frameworks that make such missions possible. For educators using [LearnOSTEAM](/work/learnosteam) or similar platforms, this mission provides real-world context for robotics, sensors, and scientific experimentation — the same principles that a payload specialist applies in orbit can be taught in simplified form in a classroom.

The announcement of a Space Museum at the University of Narowal is a step in the right direction, though its impact will depend on execution. A well-designed space museum can serve as a regional hub for STEAM education, providing hands-on exhibits, telescope access, and interactive demonstrations that go beyond what most Pakistani schools can offer.

## Product Builder's Perspective

From a product-building perspective, the China-Pakistan space cooperation model offers several lessons.

**Leverage partnerships for capability jumps.** Pakistan did not build a crewed spacecraft or a space station. Instead, it negotiated access to China's existing infrastructure, contributing a payload specialist and scientific experiments rather than hardware. This is a pragmatic approach that allowed Pakistan to enter human spaceflight decades faster than if it had tried to develop indigenous launch and crewed spacecraft capabilities. For technology teams in Pakistan, the parallel is clear: partnering with established platforms can accelerate capability acquisition in ways that building from scratch cannot.

**The payload specialist model is efficient.** By focusing on science rather than piloting or station operations, Pakistan's astronaut contributes value that leverages Pakistan's scientific community rather than its (nonexistent) astronaut corps. This is the same principle that successful product teams use: focus on what you uniquely bring to the table, and rely on partners for the rest.

**Long-term planning matters.** The 2035 lunar landing target may seem distant, but it is the kind of multi-decade vision that shapes infrastructure investment, university curricula, and industrial policy. Pakistan's rover on Chang'e-8 in 2028 will be a stepping stone. For product builders, the lesson is that ambitious goals require sequencing — each mission builds capability and institutional knowledge for the next.

## What to Watch Next

- **Candidate selection**: Watch for the announcement of which of the two candidates — Khurram Daud or Muhammad Zeeshan Ali — will fly the mission.
- **Shenzhou-24 launch on November 25**: The launch from Jiuquan will be the moment of truth. China's Long March-2F rocket has a strong safety record, but any crewed launch carries risk.
- **Science results**: The specific experiments conducted and their preliminary results may not be published immediately, but watch for SUPARCO press releases in the weeks following the mission.
- **Chang'e-8 rover development**: Pakistan's 35-kilogram lunar rover for the 2028 mission is the next major milestone. Watch for updates on its design, payload selection, and testing.
- **Space Museum at Narowal**: If executed well, this could become a meaningful STEAM education facility. Watch for construction timelines and exhibit plans.
- **ISRO's response**: India's space agency has its own ambitious human spaceflight program (Gaganyaan). The Pakistani mission may accelerate regional competition in space capability.

## Conclusion

Pakistan's first astronaut mission to Tiangong is not just a symbolic milestone. It is the product of a deliberate strategy to leverage China's space infrastructure for Pakistani scientific and strategic objectives. The November 25 launch will be the visible result; the less visible work — the training, the experiment design, the rover development for Chang'e-8, the 2035 lunar ambition — will determine whether this mission becomes the start of a sustained Pakistani human spaceflight program or remains a one-time achievement.

For Pakistani students, educators, and technology builders, the question is not whether the mission succeeds — it is what the country does with the inspiration, data, and partnerships that follow.

What would you do with access to a microgravity laboratory? For Pakistani researchers and educators, that question just became real.
