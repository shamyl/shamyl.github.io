Agility Robotics Goes Public: What the First Humanoid Robot IPO Means for the Industry

#robotics#humanoid-robot#Agility Robotics#SPAC#IPO#Digit

On November 2, 2026, shareholders of Churchill Capital Corp XI voted overwhelmingly to approve a business combination with Agility Robotics, the Salem, Oregon-based maker of the Digit humanoid robot. The transaction is expected to close on November 4, and the combined company will begin trading on the Nasdaq Stock Market under the ticker symbol “AGLT” on November 5. This marks the first time a pure-play humanoid robotics company with active commercial deployments will be publicly traded in the United States — a milestone that offers a rare, structured look at the financial reality behind the humanoid robotics hype.

In Brief

  • Churchill Capital Corp XI shareholders approved the $2.5 billion business combination with Agility Robotics on November 2, 2026, with over 84% of votes cast in favor
  • The transaction is expected to generate approximately $465 million in gross capital: ~$164 million from Churchill XI’s trust, ~$201 million from a PIPE led by Foxconn, and ~$100 million in prior equity funding
  • Agility’s common stock and warrants will trade on Nasdaq under ticker symbols “AGLT” and “AGLTW” starting November 5, 2026
  • The company has disclosed more than $300 million in committed multi-year orders for Digit 5, with an active pipeline of over 30 prospective customers including GXO, AGCO, Toyota, and Schaeffler
  • Digit 5, unveiled September 15, 2026, is scheduled for early-access deliveries in the first half of 2027 and general availability by end of 2027
  • Strategic investors include NVIDIA, Amazon, SoftBank Vision Fund 2, Schaeffler, Foxconn, Abico, DCVC, and Playground Global

What the SPAC Deal Actually Includes

According to Agility Robotics’ press release dated November 2, 2026, Churchill XI shareholders approved the business combination at a special meeting, with more than 84% of votes cast voting in favor. The deal values Agility at approximately $2.5 billion.

The gross capital raise breaks down into three components, as detailed in the company’s October 30 announcement:

  • ~$164 million from Churchill XI’s trust account
  • ~$201 million from a common stock PIPE (private investment in public equity) committed at $10 per share, led by Foxconn with participation from existing and new institutional investors
  • ~$100 million in equity funding Agility had already received prior to the transaction

Upon closing, all existing Agility shareholders will roll their equity into the combined company, subject to a 180-day lock-up period. Churchill XI will change its jurisdiction of incorporation from the Cayman Islands to Delaware and will be renamed Agility Robotics, Inc.

The Customer Base: Who Is Actually Deploying Digit?

One of the most informative aspects of Agility’s public disclosure is the level of detail about its commercial pipeline. The October 26 commercial update paints a picture of a company that has moved beyond demo videos into real, if still small-scale, deployment.

GXO Logistics. GXO has been deploying Digit at its Georgia facility since 2024 and has now signed a commercial agreement to expand to two additional facilities in Statesville, North Carolina, and Lebanon, Tennessee. This makes GXO the first customer to expand Digit deployments across multiple sites — an important signal for whether the product works well enough to scale within a single organization.

AGCO. On October 20, 2026, Agility announced a commercial agreement with AGCO for a manufacturing pilot at AGCO’s Hesston, Kansas facility, expected to begin in 2027. Digit will initially handle parts sorting, stacking, and intelligent placement of totes for storage — tasks chosen for their repetitive and physically demanding nature. Thomas Enghof, AGCO’s Director of Manufacturing Technology, framed the partnership as viewing humanoid robots as “teammates” that “absorb repetitive, strenuous or hazardous tasks.”

Toyota. Agility previously announced a robots-as-a-service deal with Toyota Motor Manufacturing Canada for deploying Digit at a Canadian assembly plant.

Three unnamed manufacturing customers. Agility disclosed three additional manufacturing customers participating in paid programs, though these companies have chosen not to be identified publicly. All three are focused on specific manufacturing needs and are using the engagements to evaluate broader deployments.

Schaeffler. The German automotive and industrial supplier is listed among Agility’s existing customers and strategic investors.

In total, Agility reports an active pipeline of more than 30 prospective customers across manufacturing, automotive, aerospace, defense, logistics, and consumer products. The company also disclosed that two potential subcontractor engagements supporting a branch of the U.S. Armed Services are in early-stage discussions.

Digit 5: The Product Behind the Orders

Digit 5, unveiled on September 15, 2026, represents Agility’s next-generation humanoid robot. According to the company, it is designed for “cooperatively safe” work in close proximity to people and can support a broader range of industrial workflows than previous generations.

Key details from Agility’s disclosures:

  • Early-access deliveries to customers are expected in the first half of 2027
  • General availability is targeted for the end of 2027
  • Agility has disclosed more than $300 million in committed multi-year orders for Digit 5
  • The company notes that this figure represents potential multi-year value subject to contractual milestones, product features, and specifications — not current-period revenue

Agility’s manufacturing infrastructure includes RoboFab, a 70,000-square-foot facility in Salem, Oregon, designed for annual production capacity of up to 10,000 robots. The company also operates Agility Arc, a cloud-based platform for managing robot fleets and integrating them into customer workflows.

The Revenue Reality

While Agility has not been shy about sharing customer wins and order figures, the SEC filings required for the SPAC transaction revealed a more modest revenue picture. According to The Robot Report, Agility reported approximately $1.8 million in revenue ahead of the merger — a figure that, while not unusual for a hardware company in the pre-production stage, underscores the gap between committed orders and recognized revenue.

This is worth contextualizing. Agility is not a software company where revenue scales linearly with users. It is a hardware company that needs to manufacture, ship, deploy, and support physical robots — each of which costs tens of thousands of dollars to produce. The $300 million in committed orders is a forward-looking indicator, but the $1.8 million in actual revenue tells you where the company is today: still in early deployment, not mass production.

For comparison, Boston Dynamics’ Atlas program at Hyundai’s RMAC is further from commercial deployment but backed by Hyundai’s manufacturing infrastructure. Figure’s Helix 2.5 has demonstrated impressive generalization in home environments but is pre-revenue. Agility’s position — modest revenue, real customers, a shipping product — places it in a different stage of the commercial maturity curve.

Why This Matters

The public listing matters for several reasons that extend beyond investor interest.

Transparency. Public companies must file regular reports with the SEC, including quarterly earnings, annual reports, and material event disclosures. This means that for the first time, the humanoid robotics industry will have a company whose financials, customer counts, deployment metrics, and operational challenges are visible on a regular cadence. For an industry that has been dominated by carefully curated demo videos and selective disclosures, this is a meaningful shift.

Price discovery. A public market valuation provides a benchmark for the entire humanoid robotics sector. Private companies like Figure, Apptronik, and Unitree will now be measured — at least implicitly — against Agility’s public market performance. This affects their ability to raise capital, the terms they negotiate, and the expectations set on their own paths to liquidity.

Capital access. The $465 million in gross capital, if deployed effectively, gives Agility the resources to scale manufacturing, expand its commercial team, and invest in the software infrastructure needed to support fleet deployments. Whether that capital is sufficient depends on the company’s burn rate, production costs, and the pace at which customers convert from pilots to larger orders.

What Could Go Wrong

A SPAC listing at this stage of commercial maturity carries real risks.

The SPAC structure has a mixed track record for early-stage companies. While it provides faster access to public markets than a traditional IPO, it also exposes companies to public market volatility and short-term performance pressure before they have reached profitability. The 180-day lock-up period will eventually expire, and existing shareholders may sell — potentially putting downward pressure on the stock.

Agility’s revenue is currently small relative to its valuation. A $2.5 billion valuation on $1.8 million in revenue implies a price-to-sales ratio that is extraordinarily high, even by growth-stock standards. The market will need to see revenue growth materialize quickly to justify that valuation.

The humanoid robotics competitive landscape is intensifying. Goldman Sachs has forecast 6.5 million humanoid robots deployed by 2035, but multiple companies are chasing that market simultaneously. Boston Dynamics has Hyundai’s backing, Figure has raised billions, Tesla is developing Optimus, and Chinese manufacturers like Unitree are producing capable robots at lower price points. Agility’s first-mover advantage in public markets does not guarantee a first-mover advantage in commercial deployment.

For Product Builders and Technology Teams

For founders and product teams watching the humanoid robotics space, Agility’s public disclosure offers several practical takeaways.

Manufacturing scale is the bottleneck, not AI. Agility’s capital is directed toward RoboFab, fleet management software, and commercial expansion — not just model training. The lesson for hardware startups is that production capacity, quality control, and deployment support often matter more than algorithmic improvements in determining which companies capture market share.

Customer expansion within existing accounts is the key metric. GXO’s expansion from one facility to three is more meaningful than a new logo. It demonstrates that the product delivers enough value for a customer to increase its commitment. Watch for similar patterns with AGCO and Toyota.

The robots-as-a-service model is still unproven at scale. Agility’s deal with Toyota was structured as a service agreement, and the company’s paid pilot programs suggest a consultative sales cycle. This is closer to enterprise software sales than to consumer electronics — long evaluation periods, custom integration, and relationship-heavy account management.

For STEAM educators and school leaders, the public listing of a humanoid robotics company is a reminder that the field is moving from research lab to factory floor faster than many curricula can adapt. Educational robotics platforms that teach students about manipulation, perception, and human-robot interaction are becoming more relevant as companies like Agility demonstrate real commercial deployment. The gap between what students learn and what the industry needs is narrowing, but it has not closed.

For Pakistani Technology Teams

Pakistan’s robotics ecosystem is at a much earlier stage, but there are relevant lessons. The capital intensity required to build and deploy humanoid robots at scale is significant — Agility’s $465 million raise is just the latest round. Pakistani technology teams interested in robotics should consider where they can add value without competing directly on hardware manufacturing: software infrastructure for fleet management, domain-specific applications for logistics or agriculture, and integration services for companies deploying imported robots.

The Physical Intelligence Pi API offers one entry point, allowing teams to fine-tune robotics foundation models without building their own hardware from scratch. For product builders in emerging markets, the strategy of leveraging open-source robotics software and affordable hardware platforms remains the most viable path.

What to Watch Next

  • November 5, 2026: AGLT begins trading on Nasdaq. The first week of trading will provide an initial market read on investor appetite for humanoid robotics.
  • First quarterly earnings report: Agility’s first earnings as a public company will be the first detailed look at post-merger financials, including revenue trajectory and cash burn.
  • Digit 5 early-access deliveries (H1 2027): Whether Agility meets its delivery timeline will be a critical credibility test.
  • Customer conversion rate: Watch how many of the 30+ prospective customers in the pipeline convert to signed agreements, and how many existing customers expand deployments.
  • Competitive response: Boston Dynamics, Figure, and Tesla may accelerate their own commercial timelines in response to Agility’s public market presence.

Conclusion

Agility Robotics’ public listing is a milestone for the humanoid robotics industry, not because it proves the technology is ready for mass deployment, but because it introduces a level of financial transparency that has been absent from a field dominated by private companies and controlled demonstrations. The $465 million in capital, the $300 million in committed orders, and the 65,000+ hours of real-world deployment data give Agility a genuine head start. But the gap between $1.8 million in revenue and a $2.5 billion valuation is a reminder that the humanoid robotics market is still in its earliest commercial stage. The next twelve months will tell us whether that gap is closing — or whether it is more stubborn than the demos suggest.
